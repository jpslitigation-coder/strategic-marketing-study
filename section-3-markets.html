Understanding Markets & Segments

Strategy concepts: drift & space

Strategic driftWhen a firm fails to keep pace with environmental change. It drifts, enters flux (instability), and may or may not manage the transformational "step change" back into alignment.
Strategic spaceOpportunities (and threats) outside the traditional competitive "box". Many firms never see them.
Practical example — recorded musicThe market wasn't reconfigured by record labels but by artists selling direct, iTunes downloads, the iPod/phones, and subscription streaming. Incumbents were left hunting for profit in a wholly reconfigured space — classic strategic drift out of strategic space.

What exactly is a market?

MarketGroups of people or businesses with a need who have the ability and willingness to buy. A product-market combines a product's benefits with the needs that motivate demand.

Ford Taurus and Toyota Camry share a market because they are substitutes — either can satisfy the same need. Define a product-market broadly enough to include all categories competing for the same buyer need.

Business vs consumer markets

B2BB2C
DecisionSeveral people (buyer, user, agent/management); rationalIndividual decides; more emotional
Sales cycleLengthy, relationship-drivenShorter, less complex
ApproachPersonal — demos, many meetingsMass groups & mass media
Practical example — the photocopierThe user wants it fast and easy (sort, staple, multiple trays). The finance manager weighs capital outlay and running costs (ROI). The buyer wants the lease/maintenance paperwork right. Management makes the rational call, but users exert emotional influence.

Market segmentation

Market segmentA distinct subset of people with similar needs, circumstances and characteristics that lead them to respond similarly to a product offering or marketing programme (Walker & Mullins, 2014).

Three steps (Cravens & Piercy)

Memory hook · bases for segmenting
"Big Dogs Get Bones"
  • Behavioural — usage, benefits sought, online vs in-store, loyalty
  • Demographic — age, sex, income, occupation, education, race; generational (Gen Y/Z)
  • Geographic — region, climate, location
  • Behavioural & psychographic (VALS) — lifestyle, values, motivations

B2B: macro then micro. Macro-segmentation splits by the buying organisation's traits (age of firm, size, industry). Micro-segmentation then groups by the people who influence the decision. Finer, "sliver" segments are viable thanks to technology (micromultinationals).

Practical example — Best Buy personasBest Buy built named segments — Jill (busy suburban mum), Barry (affluent professional), Buzz (young tech enthusiast), Mr Storefront (small business). Staff are trained to recognise each shopper and match benefits to their needs.

Evaluating segments

Not every segment is worth pursuing. Score each on two factor-sets, weight × rate (0–10), then plot on the matrix.

Market-attractiveness factors

  • Unmet customer needs
  • Segment size & growth rate
  • Macro trends

Competitive-position factors

  • Opportunity for competitive advantage
  • Capabilities & resources
  • Industry attractiveness (Porter's 5 forces)
Practical example — Under ArmourUnder Armour's athletic-underwear segment scored ~8.6 attractiveness and ~5.4 position → top-middle "desirable potential target". The team pursues it actively, hoping to move it rightward (toward "protect position").

Selecting a target: five strategies

Memory hook · targeting strategies
"Some Sharp Players Make Fortunes"
  • Single-segment (concentrated) — one segment, one mix (small firms)
  • Selective specialisation (differentiated) — different mixes for different segments
  • Product specialisation — one product tailored across segments
  • Market specialisation — many products for one segment
  • Full market coverage — one mass mix for the whole market
Watch out · faulty segmentationTalbots chased "thirtysomethings" with frilly pink tank tops and alienated its loyal pearl-wearing career women — sales and share price fell. Segmentation lays the groundwork for targeting and positioning; get it wrong and both fail.

Customer relationship management (CRM)

CRMA comprehensive strategy/process of acquiring, retaining and partnering with selective customers to create superior value for both company and customer. It is strategic — not just technology. 60–80% of CRM projects fail, mostly from poor conceptualisation.

Three CRM pitfalls

  • Implementing CRM before creating a customer strategy.
  • Putting CRM in place before changing structure/systems/culture to match.
  • Assuming more technology is automatically better — tech must support the strategy, not lead it.

Marketing intelligence (MIS)

Marketing intelligence combines quantitative (hard) and qualitative (soft) data from primary and secondary sources to inform strategic decisions. Big data is a double-edged sword.

Exam link · LO 6 (MIS ethics)Big data improves operational/strategic risk but can damage reputational risk (the "creep factor"). No database is perfectly anonymous; patterns aren't reality; profiling can discriminate. Buytendijk & Heiser recommend an ethical code of conduct for the marketing team rather than relying on regulation alone.

Section key points

  • Markets = groups with a need plus the ability and willingness to buy (ability + willingness = demand).
  • Segmentation follows three steps (identify → specify → determine size) using demographic/geographic/psychographic/behavioural bases; B2B uses macro then micro.
  • Evaluate segments on market-attractiveness × competitive-position, then plot on the matrix.
  • Five targeting strategies exist; faulty segmentation alienates customers and weakens positioning.
  • CRM (engage → segment → personalise → track) develops long-term, loyal customers; 60–80% of projects fail.
  • Marketing intelligence blends hard/soft and primary/secondary data — govern big data with an ethical code of conduct.
